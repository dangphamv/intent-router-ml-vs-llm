  • How is val different from test? Val: you may look at the score and then make changes. Test: you may not. Once you pick things based on a set's score, that set is no longer honest, so you must keep one set that nobody has looked at.
  • With cross-validation, where is val? Merge train + val, split into 5 parts; each round learns on 4 parts and takes a practice exam on 1 part — that practice part is the "val", done 5 times to be safe. Test still stays outside.
  • Why does the 90.6 score use a model trained on train, while the production model is trained on train + val? The score must be measured by a model that has never seen test, using the approach that was already locked in. Once scoring is done, val is added so the model learns from more data before the restaurant opens.
  • What if you peeked at test and then made changes? A fake-good score: choosing the threshold on test gives 95.1 instead of the real 93.1 — that is data leakage.
Self-check: true or false? (click to see the answers)
  1. The test set has no labels. → False: it has labels, otherwise it couldn't be scored.
  2. You may use val to choose between logistic regression and XGBoost. → True.
  3. If the test score is low, change C and score test again. → False: that is leakage; make changes based on val.
  4. Scoring 100 on train means the model is very good. → False: it may just have memorised; look at val / test.

How do the 4 approaches work?

A, B and C all do 2 steps: (1) turn the sentence into a list of numbers (called features) → (2) a classification model learns from 1,400 labelled messages to guess the label. D skips both steps and asks the LLM directly.

The assignment also puts B and C in the "classical models on embeddings" group. What A, B and C have in common: they must learn from labelled data, they run fast and cheaply, and they return a probability for each label — the probability is what makes the "answer it yourself when confident" strategy possible.

A. TF-IDF + logistic regression — counting text

Step 1 — TF-IDF. Break the sentence into pieces and count them:

  • Words and word pairs: huy, goi, huy goi…
  • Chunks of 2–5 letters: hu, huy, uy g… → this way "huy goi" (no diacritics), "hủy gói" and "huỷ gói" (two spellings of "cancel plan") still share many pieces.
  • Pieces that appear in every message (like "mình" ("I/me"), "ạ" (a politeness particle)) get a lower weight; rare but telling pieces (like "hủy" ("cancel"), "giá" ("price")) get a higher weight. That is what "TF-IDF" means.

Result: each message becomes a list of tens of thousands of numbers, most of them 0.

Step 2 — Logistic regression. Learns a weight for each piece for each label. For example "hủy" ("cancel") has a high weight for cancellation, and "lỗi" ("error") a high weight for tech_support. Add up the weights of the pieces in the sentence → get a probability for each of the 5 labels.

Settings: 1–2 word n-grams + 2–5 character chunks (char_wb), C=10 (how closely the model is allowed to fit the data), class_weight="balanced" (more weight for rare classes).

Strengths: instant, free, works offline, handles messages without diacritics well. Weaknesses: only sees text, doesn't understand meaning — "ngưng dịch vụ" ("stop the service") and "hủy gói" ("cancel plan") use different words, so both must appear in the training data.

B. Embedding + logistic regression — measuring meaning

Step 1 — Embedding. Send the sentence to OpenAI's text-embedding-3-small API (a neural network already trained on a huge amount of text). The API returns 1,536 numbers that represent the sentence's meaning. Two sentences with the same meaning produce two lists of numbers that are close together, even if they share no words: "hủy gói" ("cancel plan") ≈ "không dùng nữa, ngưng giúp mình" ("not using it anymore, please stop it for me").

Step 2 — Logistic regression, same as A, but it learns on these 1,536 numbers instead of word counts.

Strengths: understands synonyms and new ways of phrasing things. Weaknesses: each message costs one network call (~0.2–0.3 seconds) and a little money; strong emotion in a sentence easily "pulls" its meaning towards complaint.

C. Embedding + XGBoost — many decision trees

Step 1: reuses exactly B's embeddings.

Step 2 — XGBoost: builds 400 decision trees one after another; each tree is a chain of questions like "is number 37 greater than 0.02?…", and each new tree focuses on fixing the mistakes of the trees before it. The final result is the combined vote of all 400 trees.

Settings: 400 trees, max depth 4, learning_rate=0.08, each tree only sees 80% of the messages and 40% of the columns.

Result: no better than B. XGBoost is strong on tabular data (age, income…), but with 1,536 dense numbers and only 1,400 training examples, a linear model like logistic regression is enough — and XGBoost handles the rare class worst.

D. Zero-shot LLM — just ask

Send the message to gpt-4o-mini along with a short description of the 5 labels, and ask it to return exactly one label. "Zero-shot" means no examples and no training. temperature=0 so that every time you ask, you get the same answer.

Strengths: understands context, tone, and messages with several intents. Weaknesses: slow (~0.7–0.9 seconds/message), costs money per message, and doesn't know your own rules (e.g. "billed incorrectly → complaint, not pricing") unless you write them into the prompt.

Picture a receptionist and an expert:

  • The receptionist (model A) reads the message first. Clear messages like "Gói Pro bao nhiêu tiền?" ("How much is the Pro plan?") or "Hủy gói giúp mình" ("Please cancel my plan") get answered right away, at no cost.
  • When the receptionist is unsure (confidence ≤ 72%), for example "Hơi thất vọng, thôi hủy luôn cho đơn giản" ("A bit disappointed, I'll just cancel to keep it simple"), the message is passed to the expert (LLM).
  • Result: only 1/4 of messages have to be paid for with the LLM, while the quality is almost the same as sending everything to the LLM.

This works because the two sides make mistakes in different places: of the 29 messages A got wrong, the LLM got 24 right.

The cost–quality curve

4 takeaways

  1. A, B and C are on par. The gap between the three (88–91 points) is smaller than the measurement error, so none is a clear winner. The simplest one (A) is good enough.
  2. The LLM is about 4 points better, but about 1,000 times slower and about 50 times more expensive than B.
  3. The most common error isn't the model's fault. Every approach, including the LLM, often confuses "other" and "tech support". For example, which category "Có tính năng đổi mã dự án không?" ("Is there a feature to change the project code?") belongs to is hard even for a human to say. To improve, clarify the label definitions first.
  4. The scariest error is missing a customer who wants to cancel. Messages that both complain and demand cancellation are easily misclassified as "complaint". Balancing the weights between classes clearly reduces this error.

Glossary

Accuracy score
The macro-F1 metric times 100. A score is computed for each intent and then averaged, so intents with few messages (like "cancellation") matter just as much as intents with many.
TF-IDF
Turns a message into a table of word and letter-chunk counts. Thanks to the letter-chunk counts, the model still understands messages without diacritics like "huy goi" (= "hủy gói", "cancel plan").
Embedding
Uses an API to turn a sentence into a list of 1,536 numbers that represent the sentence's meaning. Costs one network call per message.
Logistic regression / XGBoost
Two kinds of models that learn from labelled examples. Logistic regression is simpler; XGBoost is more complex but is not better on this task.
Zero-shot LLM
Ask a large language model directly "which category is this message?", no training needed.
Confidence threshold
The cheap model only answers on its own when its confidence is above the threshold (72%); below it, the message goes to the LLM.
p95
95% of messages are answered faster than this number.

TF-IDF with real numbers

  • TF (term frequency): how many times a word appears in this message. "hủy gói, hủy luôn" ("cancel the plan, cancel it right now") → "hủy" ("cancel") has TF = 2.
  • IDF (rarity): how many messages in the whole dataset contain the word — the fewer messages contain it, the more "noteworthy" it is.
WordMessages containing the word (out of 1,700)IDFMeaning
có ("have / is there")6531.96too common → little value

A word's score in a message = TF × IDF. That's why "hủy" ("cancel") weighs almost twice as much as "có" ("have"), even though both appear only once in the sentence. The IDF numbers come from the saved model (models/tfidf_lr.joblib).

Q&A for beginners

Common questions when you're first learning this project, with short answers and real numbers. Click each question to see the answer.

Data
What does "hand-label ~300, label the rest with an LLM, then spot-check a sample" mean?

A label is the correct answer for each message (which category the message belongs to). Hand-labelling all 2,000 messages would take too long, so: a person carefully labels 300 messages as the "answer key" → the LLM labels the remaining 1,700 → 100 messages the LLM labelled are picked at random for a person to review. If ≥ 90% are correct, the rest can be trusted. It's like a teacher marking a few sample papers, handing the rest to a teaching assistant, and then spot-checking at random. See Input.

Were all 1,700 messages labelled?

Yes. The LLM wrote 2,135 messages, and after removing duplicates exactly 1,700 were left; gpt-4o labelled all 1,700. Of those, 100 were checked by a person and 5 had their label corrected. Total dataset: 300 hand-labelled + 1,700 LLM = 2,000 messages.

What is audit_sample.csv?

"Audit" = check. These are 100 messages picked at random from the 1,700 the LLM labelled, with 3 columns: text (the message), llm_label (the LLM's label), human_label (the person's label). The script compares the two columns: 95/100 match = 95%, and it fixes the label in the dataset for messages where the person disagreed. For example, the LLM saw the words "cắt dịch vụ" ("cut off the service") and labelled it cancellation, but the customer was complaining about being cut off without notice → the person changed it to complaint.

How are train, val and test different?

All 3 sets have labels of the same quality — only their purpose differs. Think of exam prep: train (1,400) = a workbook with worked solutions, the model learns from it; val (300) = a practice exam, used to choose how to do things (e.g. choose the threshold); test (300) = the real exam, used only once for the final score. If you use test to choose how to do things, the score will be "fake-good" (see data leakage).

In this project, 300 val messages are too few, so the threshold is chosen with cross-validation on train + val (1,700 messages) — like taking 5 practice exams instead of 1.

What is val for? Why not just score on train or test?

Val gives you an honest score while you are still allowed to change your approach.

  • Scoring on train is meaningless — the model has already seen the answers. Model A scores 100 points on train (memorised), but only 88.5 on val and 90.6 on test. Like a student redoing exercises from a book with worked solutions: always right, but it tells you nothing.
  • Using test to choose makes test dishonest — choosing the threshold on test makes the reported score 95.1 instead of the real 93.1.
  • Val sits in between: the model hasn't learned from it (so the score is honest), and you are allowed to look at the score to make decisions. Test is saved for the final scoring.

Example of choosing a parameter with val — logistic regression's C parameter (the bigger C is, the more closely the model sticks to the training data):

CScore on trainScore on val
0.192.987.4
199.186.9

Looking at train, C = 10 and C = 100 both score 100 — you can't tell them apart, and it's easy to wrongly pick the biggest C. Val shows C = 10 is best; C = 100 "memorises" too much, so it does worse on new messages (this is called overfitting). The value C = 10 the project uses is exactly the best value on val.

Val's limitation: 300 messages is small, so the score fluctuates — a 1-point difference may just be luck. That's why, for the router threshold, the project uses cross-validation (5 practice exams on 1,700 messages) to be safer.

Val is used to make "decisions" — which decisions exactly?

They are the choices about how to do things that must be locked in before the final scoring on test (collectively called choosing the model and hyperparameters):

The general pattern: try an option → learn on train → score on val → pick the best option → (retrain on train + val) → score on test once.

If val is the practice exam, does it change the training set? Where are the results saved?

The training data is not changed. A practice exam doesn't edit the workbook — it helps you choose how to study (called settings: threshold, model parameters…). The data in data/intents.jsonl stays the same.

  • Try many settings: for each setting, learn on train → take the exam on val → record the score.
  • Pick the setting with the best score.
  • Usually retrain once more on train + val with the chosen setting (more data).
  • Score on test exactly once.

Are the 300 test messages taken from the 1,700 LLM-generated messages?

Models
Are A, B and C all classical ML?

Yes, but to different degrees. The label-guessing part of all three is classical (logistic regression, XGBoost). As for the sentence-to-numbers part: A uses TF-IDF (classical), while B and C use embeddings from a neural network. So A is fully classical; B and C are "classical models on embeddings" — exactly what the assignment calls them. Only D is an LLM. See How the 4 approaches work.

Where are the classical models saved?

Only model A is saved: models/tfidf_lr.joblib (1.25 MB), containing the model (TF-IDF + logistic regression, retrained on train + val), the threshold 0.72 and the list of labels. The router reads this file. B and C aren't saved — every time the script/notebook runs, they are retrained in a few seconds from the cached embeddings.

import joblib
b = joblib.load("models/tfidf_lr.joblib")
p = b["model"].predict_proba(["Hủy gói giúp mình"])[0]
print(b["model"].classes_[p.argmax()], p.max())
Where is the text-embedding-3-small model called?

The model runs on OpenAI's servers and the project calls it through the API — it isn't downloaded to your machine.

  • The model name is declared in src/intent_router/config.py (EMBED_MODEL, can be changed in .env).
  • The API is called in src/intent_router/embed.py: embed_texts() embeds the 2,000 messages in batches of 256 and caches them; measure_latency() sends messages one by one to measure speed.
  • Used in scripts/run_experiments.py, scripts/rare_class_ablation.py and the notebook — messages already in data/cache/embeddings_*.npz aren't sent again.
After embedding, where is the data stored?

In a single file on disk: data/cache/embeddings_text-embedding-3-small.npz (5.8 MB). No database or vector database is used.

.npz is the compressed format of the numpy library, like a zip file holding 2 arrays side by side:

ArraySizeContents
texts2,000the original sentence, e.g. "Gói Pro có bao nhiêu seat?" ("How many seats does the Pro plan have?")
vectors2,000 × 1,536the vector for each sentence, e.g. [-0.024, -0.023, 0.037, …] (1,536 numbers)

Row i of texts matches row i of vectors. The numbers are stored as float16 (just enough precision) so the file is half the size.

At run time: embed_texts() reads the file → messages already there are used as-is, missing ones are sent to the API and appended to the file → it returns a table of numbers for logistic regression / XGBoost to use in memory.

import numpy as np
z = np.load("data/cache/embeddings_text-embedding-3-small.npz")
print(z["texts"][0], z["vectors"][0][:6])

Other files in data/cache/: embed_latency_*.jsonl (time + token count for each embedding call), zeroshot_gpt-4o-mini.jsonl (D's answers), annotator_gpt-4o.jsonl (labels assigned by the LLM), generated_raw.jsonl (the 2,135 messages the LLM wrote).

Is the embedding in B and C the same as RAG's ingest step?

Creating the embeddings is exactly the same (same model, same API: sentence → 1,536 numbers). How they're used is different:

  • RAG: cut documents into chunks → embed → store in a vector database. When a question comes in, embed the question, then search for the closest chunks and hand them to the LLM. Embedding = an index for looking things up, no labels needed.
  • B, C: embed the message → use the 1,536 numbers as input to a model that learns together with the labels → guess the intent. No vector database, no search.

The leakage check (finding the train message most similar to each test message) is exactly RAG's "retrieval" operation. The full ingest part comes up in project #2.

Does approach D just send the 300 test messages straight to ChatGPT, without using train/val?

The idea is right, with just 2 differences in detail:

  • It's not ChatGPT (the chat app) but OpenAI's API, model gpt-4o-mini, called from code (classify() in src/intent_router/llm.py). Each message is a separate call, temperature=0, and the LLM is forced to return 1 of the 5 labels.
  • Each call includes a short description of the 5 labels (the prompt below) — no examples at all from train/val. That's what "zero-shot" means: the LLM learns nothing from our data and relies only on what it already knows.
Classify the customer message sent to a SaaS project-management product's support channel.
Return exactly one label: pricing, complaint, cancellation, tech_support, other.
- pricing: questions about prices, plans, quotas, discounts, payment methods
- complaint: dissatisfaction, billing errors, slow support, demands for refunds
- cancellation: cancel, downgrade, pause, stop renewal, delete/close account
- tech_support: how-to questions, bugs, login, integrations, API
- other: greetings, thanks, feature ideas, jobs, partnerships, off-topic

D's score of 94.3 is computed on the 300 test messages. But in practice D was also run on the 1,700 train + val messages — not to learn, but to simulate the hybrid strategy while choosing the threshold (you need to know "if this message is sent to the LLM, what would the LLM answer"). 2,000 calls in total, with results saved in data/cache/zeroshot_gpt-4o-mini.jsonl.

Compared with A, B, C: those three must learn from the 1,400 labelled training messages; D doesn't learn, it just "reads the question and answers".

Is the confidence threshold the same as the threshold in RAG?

Not quite. Both are thresholds — a "trustworthy enough?" cut-off — but they measure two different things:

Confidence threshold (this project)Similarity threshold (RAG)
What it measuresHow sure the model is about the label it guessedHow similar a document chunk is to the question
Where the number comes fromThe highest probability among the 5 labels (predict_proba)Cosine similarity between the question's vector and the chunk's vector
Example"Hủy gói giúp mình" ("Please cancel my plan") → cancellation 0.98"Chính sách hoàn tiền?" ("Refund policy?") ↔ "Hoàn tiền trong 7 ngày…" ("Refunds within 7 days…") (illustrative example)
Below the thresholdSend the message to the LLMDrop that chunk; if no chunk passes the threshold → answer "no information found"

They are chosen the same way: try many thresholds on val, plot the trade-off curve, and choose with a reason. Router: higher threshold → more LLM calls, more accurate but more expensive. RAG: higher threshold → fewer off-topic chunks but more likely to miss the right chunk (precision ↔ recall).

This project does use one RAG-style threshold: in the data-leakage check, test–train pairs with cosine similarity > 0.9 are treated as near-duplicates (result: 0 pairs, highest 0.899). Similarity thresholds depend on the embedding model, so if you change the model you must measure and choose again.

What is the notebook for? How do I open it correctly?

A notebook (an .ipynb file) is a document that mixes code + results + explanatory text. The code is split into cells; you run them one by one, and the results (tables, charts) show up right below — like a lab notebook where you write down the method and see the results at the same time.

Where do the scores 90.6 / 88.2 / 89.6 / 94.3 / 93.1 come from?

From the 300 test messages — the models never learned from these. Each approach guesses labels for the 300 messages, they are compared with the correct labels, and then macro-F1 is computed (F1 for each label, then averaged over the 5 labels) × 100. Do embeddings cost money?

Yes, but very little: $0.02 / 1 million tokens, ~29 tokens per message. The whole project embedded ~71,000 tokens ≈ $0.0014 (about 35 VND). In production, ~$0.58 / 1 million messages — about 55 times cheaper than the LLM (~$32). The real price of B and C is waiting time (+0.2–0.3 seconds/message) and needing a network connection + API key. Rerunning the notebook costs nothing extra because of the cache.

So which approach wins in the end?

Hybrid A + D: model A answers when confidence > 72% (75% of messages), the rest go to the LLM. It scores 93.1 points (LLM-only 94.3) at a cost of $8 instead of $32 / 1 million messages. If you only care about accuracy, it's D; the cheapest and fastest is A. See the Leaderboard.

1. Precision, recall, F1 — and choosing a metric

Everyday example: casting a fishing net in a pond. The pond has 100 fish, mixed in with rubbish. You pull up the net with 50 things: 45 fish and 5 pieces of rubbish.

  • Precision (how clean the net's catch is) — of the things pulled up, how many are fish? 45 / 50 = 90%. Low precision = pulling up lots of rubbish (false alarms).
  • Recall (how much of the pond the net covers) — of all the fish in the pond, how many did you catch? 45 / 100 = 45%. Low recall = missing lots of fish.
  • F1 — a single score combining both, which is only high when both are high: 2 × 0.90 × 0.45 / (0.90 + 0.45) = 60%. Unlike the simple average (67.5%), F1 drags the score down when one side is too low — you can't "cheat" by being good at only one side.

Two ways to cheat: a tiny net that catches just 1 fish you're sure of → 100% precision but 1% recall. Dredge the whole pond → 100% recall but full of rubbish. F1 punishes both.

Why not use accuracy (the share of correct guesses)? Because the rare class gets "drowned out". The project's decision: use macro-F1 as the main metric, and separately track recall for cancellation — that's why class weight balancing is turned on and the rare-class experiment was run.

2. Data leakage

Data leakage is when information from the test set "leaks" into training or model selection. The consequence: the reported score is fake-good, and it drops when used for real. Like a student who saw the exam questions in advance: a high score that doesn't reflect real ability.

1. The data has 1% fraud. The model gets 99% accuracy. Is that good?

The simple idea

10,000 transactions, 100 of them fraud. A lazy "model" says "not fraud" for every transaction: right on 9,900/10,000 = 99% accuracy — but it catches 0 fraud cases (0% recall). Accuracy is dominated by the majority class and hides the rare class — and the rare class is exactly what you need to catch.

Numbers from the project
  • A model that never guesses "cancellation" still has 89.7% accuracy, but macro-F1 is only 76.
  • When "cancellation" is forced down to 2.9% with no weight balancing: model A only catches 57% of cancellation messages; turning on class_weight="balanced" → 72%.
  • Model A's PR-AUC for the "cancellation" label on val: 0.969 (random guessing would only get ~0.107 — the share of cancellation messages).
Sample answer
Not necessarily. With 1% fraud, a model that predicts "not fraud" for everything also gets 99% accuracy without catching a single case. I'd look at the confusion matrix, measure precision and recall for the fraud class, and PR-AUC — a metric that summarises precision/recall across all thresholds and suits imbalanced data better than ROC-AUC. To improve: class weights or resampling (on the training set only), then choose the threshold based on the real costs: how much a missed fraud costs, and how much wrongly blocking a genuine transaction costs.

2. What is data leakage? Give an example.

The simple idea

Information about the answers "leaks" in during training → a fake-good score that drops in the real world. Like a student who saw the exam questions in advance.

3 classic examples
  • Using information from the future: predicting whether a customer will churn next month, but a feature is "number of calls to the hotline to cancel service" — something that only happens after the customer has already decided to leave.
  • Normalising before splitting: computing the mean / standard deviation (or the TF-IDF vocabulary) on all the data before splitting into train/test → train has already "seen" test.
  • The same entity in both train and test: many transactions from the same customer end up in both sets → the model memorises the customer instead of learning the pattern. Fix: split by customer (GroupKFold).
Example from the project
  • If the router threshold were chosen on the test set itself, the reported score would be 95.1 instead of 93.1 — 2 fake points. The project chooses it on out-of-fold predictions from train + val.
  • LLM-generated messages can be very similar to each other → deduplicate before splitting, and check: no test–train pair is more similar than 0.9.
  • TF-IDF sits inside a Pipeline, so it only learns the vocabulary on train (and on each fold).
Sample answer
Data leakage is when information you wouldn't have at real prediction time leaks into training or model selection, so the offline score looks better than it really is. An example from my intent classification project: if I chose the threshold for routing to the LLM using the test set itself, the reported score would be 95.1, while the honest score is 93.1. I avoid that by choosing the threshold on out-of-fold predictions from train+val. I also deduplicate messages before splitting, put TF-IDF inside a Pipeline so it's only fit on train, and check the similarity between test and train. Other common forms: features taken from the future, normalising before splitting, and the same customer appearing in both train and test.

Keywords: train/test contaminationtarget leakagefit on train onlyPipelineGroupKFoldtime-based split

3. When should you use a classical model instead of an LLM?

The simple idea

A classical model is like a specialist employee who does one job: fast, cheap, good at familiar work. An LLM is like an all-round expert: understands everything, but slow and charges for every sentence. Narrow, repetitive, high-volume work → give it to the specialist employee.

Numbers from the project
  • Model A: 90.6 points, < 1 ms, ~$0. LLM: 94.3 points, ~0.9 seconds, $32 / 1 million messages.
  • The project itself follows the pattern "LLM labels the data → train a cheap model → the LLM only handles hard cases": gpt-4o assigns 1,700 labels → train model A → the router lets A handle 75% of messages and the LLM 25% → 93.1 points at $8 / 1 million messages.
Sample answer
When the task is narrow and repetitive (classifying into a few fixed labels), traffic is high, low latency is needed, and you have or can create labelled data. In my intent classification project, TF-IDF + logistic regression reached 90.6 macro-F1 in under 1 ms almost for free, while the LLM reached 94.3 but took ~0.9 seconds and $32 per million messages. In practice I usually combine them: use the LLM to label data, train a cheap model, and then send only the hard cases — where the cheap model isn't confident — to the LLM. That kept 93.1 points while cutting cost by 75%. An LLM is a better fit when the task is open-ended, data is scarce, reasoning is needed, or the task changes often.

Keywords: latencycost per requestLLM-as-labelerdistillationconfidence-based routing / cascade

4. Which matters more, precision or recall?

The simple idea

Turn the question around: which kind of mistake is more expensive? Wrongly sending your boss's email to spam (a false alarm) → you need precision. Letting a fraud case or harmful content slip through (a miss) → you need recall.

Sample answer
It depends on the cost of each kind of mistake. If false alarms are expensive — like blocking real email — prioritise precision. If misses are expensive — like fraud, harmful content, or a customer who wants to cancel but isn't handled — prioritise recall. The two trade off through the threshold: in my project, lowering the threshold for the "cancellation" label from 0.9 to 0.2 raised recall from 62% to 97%, but precision dropped from 100% to 86%. I choose the threshold by assigning a cost to each kind of mistake and taking the threshold with the lowest total cost on the validation set. If I need a single number, I use F1, or F-beta to lean towards recall.

5. The model does well on train but poorly on test. What do you do?

The simple idea

A student who memorised the worked solutions in the book but gets stuck on new exam questions — overfitting (memorising instead of understanding). Conversely, if they do badly on both the exercises and the exam — underfitting (hasn't studied enough, or the "brain" is too simple).

Sample answer
Good on train but poor on test is overfitting — the model memorises the training data instead of the pattern. First I check there's no data leakage or distribution shift between train and test. Then: add more data, reduce model complexity, increase regularization, use early stopping for iteratively trained models, and use cross-validation to choose parameters. In my intent classification project, the model scored 100 on train but 88.5 on val; growing the training data from 175 to 1,400 messages raised val from 76.9 to 88.5, and I chose the regularization level with val. If train is also poor, that's underfitting — you need better features or a stronger model.
